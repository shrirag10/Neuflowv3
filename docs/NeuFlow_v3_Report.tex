\section*{Summary}
NeuFlow v2 always computes flow for every pixel at one fixed resolution. v3
replaces only its final upsampling stage with an implicit decoder, so the model
answers flow queries at any continuous coordinate. Cost scales with the number of
points asked for, $O(N)$, not with image area, $O(H \times W)$.

v3 matches v2's accuracy with 13\% fewer parameters. It adds three things v2
cannot do at all: flow at sub-pixel coordinates, repeat queries on a cached frame
at 7.7$\times$ lower cost, and a calibrated confidence value per query.

\section{Why queryable flow}
Flow networks produce a dense map. Many consumers do not want one.

\begin{itemize}
\item Registration needs a few hundred correspondences at points it picks itself.
\item Sparse tracking needs flow only at feature points.
\item Survey mosaicking needs matches in overlap regions, often between pixels.
\end{itemize}

Computing 479{,}232 values to use 800 of them is the difference between real time
and not on constrained hardware. A fixed-resolution design also cannot answer any
query above its input sampling rate, which is what high-resolution registration
needs.

The question this project asks: can the last stage be made queryable without
losing accuracy or speed?

\section{Method}

\subsection{What is inherited}
NeuFlow v2 (Zhang, Gupta, Jiang \& Singh, arXiv:2408.10161) runs a shallow CNN
backbone at $1/8$ and $1/16$ resolution, cross-attention and global matching at
$1/16$ for an initial flow, recurrent refinement (one iteration at $1/16$, eight
at $1/8$), then a learned convex upsampler to full resolution.

v3 keeps all of that up to the $1/8$ coarse flow, with every learned weight
frozen. Only the upsampler is replaced. In the runs reported here the
batch-normalisation running statistics of the frozen stack adapted to the
training data instead of holding v2's values. Section~6 covers what that costs.

\subsection{The decoder}
Two phases.

\textbf{Phase 1, once per frame pair, 16.6\ms.} The frozen pipeline produces the
coarse flow and feature maps. These are cached.

\textbf{Phase 2, once per query batch, 2.55\ms\ for up to 2{,}048 points.} For a
coordinate $(x, y)$ the decoder samples $3\times3$ windows from four sources
($1/8$ context, $1/8$ features, $1/16$ features, flow-warped frame-1 features),
fuses them in a gated MLP, and predicts weights over ten candidates: the nine
coarse-flow values around the query plus a bilinear sample.

Output is a convex combination of those candidates. That bounds the prediction
inside the locally supported motion range, so the decoder cannot invent
displacement its inputs do not support. The head is initialised so the softmax
sits on the bilinear candidate, which makes the untrained decoder exactly
equivalent to bilinear upsampling (measured: 0.011\px\ maximum deviation).
Training can only move away from that by learning.

\subsection{Uncertainty head}
An optional eleventh output predicts a per-query error scale $b$ under a Laplace
likelihood. It costs no measurable inference time and attaches a confidence value
to every correspondence returned.

\subsection{Interface}
\begin{small}
\begin{verbatim}
state = model.infer_coarse_state(img0, img1)          # once per pair
flow  = model.decode_queries(state, query_coords=q)   # [B,N,2] -> [B,N,2]
flow  = model.decode_queries(state, target_h=H, target_w=W)
flow, b = model.decode_queries(state, query_coords=q, return_uncertainty=True)
\end{verbatim}
\end{small}

Coordinates are continuous. $(312.7, 188.2)$ is as valid as $(312, 188)$. Sparse
queries reproduce the dense field exactly at matching coordinates, to
0.00057\px.

\section{Setup}
\textbf{Evaluation.} VKITTI2 Scene18 and Scene20: 1{,}174 pairs, 460{,}573{,}660
valid pixels, per-pixel metrics. Both scenes are excluded from every training set.
A guard in the loader enforces it and I verify no overlap at pair and frame level
before each run.

\textbf{Controls.} Every training run is generated from one template, so any two
differ in exactly one variable. All use seed 1234, batch 16, learning rate
$2\times10^{-4}$ on a OneCycle schedule, $\gamma = 0.8$, 4{,}096 supervision
queries per image, refinement of $1\times$s16 and $8\times$s8 matching evaluation,
and 100{,}000 steps.

\textbf{Pre-flight.} A nine-check suite runs on CPU and GPU before any job:
batch-normalisation frozen, only decoder parameters trainable, zero-initialised
decoder equals bilinear, sparse equals dense, uncertainty present in both decode
paths, stride-2 fidelity, seed reproducibility.

\textbf{Hardware.} V100, fp16, $384\times1248$ input unless stated.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Accuracy}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
\toprule
Model & Training data & EPE (px) & 1px (\%) & 3px (\%) & Params \\
\midrule
NeuFlow v2 & FlyingThings & 2.324 & 77.63 & 89.80 & 9.03\,M \\
NeuFlow v3 & FlyingChairs & 2.286 & 71.30 & 87.57 & \textbf{7.83\,M} \\
NeuFlow v3 & \quad + VKITTI2 & 2.138 & 76.38 & 89.46 & 7.83\,M \\
NeuFlow v3 & \quad + MPI-Sintel & 2.147 & 76.81 & 89.56 & 7.83\,M \\
NeuFlow v3 & \quad + uncertainty head & \textbf{2.104} & 76.88 & 89.61 & 7.83\,M \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The FlyingChairs row is the fair comparison. That model saw no driving imagery,
which is also true of v2. It scores 2.286\px\ against v2's 2.324\px\ on unseen
driving scenes, so the two are equivalent, with v3 using 13\% fewer parameters.

The mixed-data rows reach 2.104\px, but they train on VKITTI2 scenes from the same
simulator as the test set and v2 never saw that data. Those numbers show what the
architecture reaches given representative data. They are not a fair comparison
and I do not present them as beating v2.

The three mixed configurations span 2.10 to 2.16\px\ and cannot be separated from
each other. Evaluating two checkpoints of each run moves the result by up to
0.038\px, which is the same size as the gaps between them. Nothing below roughly
0.05\px\ is resolvable here. What does hold at both checkpoints: adding in-domain
data is worth about 0.15\px\ and five points of 1-pixel accuracy.

\subsection{Compute}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
Mode & Latency & Note \\
\midrule
NeuFlow v2, full frame & 19.6\ms & its only mode \\
v3 sparse, first query on a new pair & 19.16\ms & level with v2 \\
\textbf{v3 sparse, repeat query on a cached pair} & \textbf{2.55\ms} & \textbf{7.7$\times$ cheaper} \\
v3 dense, stride 2 & 22.0\ms & not the intended mode \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The sparse path is a 16.61\ms\ coarse pass inherited from v2 plus a 2.55\ms\
decode. Global matching needs whole-image context, so the coarse pass cannot be
skipped. It is 87\% of a first query, which is why a first query costs what a v2
frame costs.

State reuse is the property that matters. v2 keeps nothing between calls, so
asking a second question about the same frame means recomputing everything. v3
answers from cached state in 2.55\ms. For anything that queries a frame more than
once (iterative registration, RANSAC refinement, interactive selection,
multi-hypothesis tracking) that is a structural difference, not a margin.

Decode latency is 2.553\ms\ at $N=800$ and 2.554\ms\ at $N=2{,}048$. The decode is
bound by kernel launch overhead, not compute, so 2{,}048 points cost the same as
800.

Measured over 2{,}348{,}000 queries. Actual error rises monotonically across all
five bins, a 21$\times$ span, Pearson $r = 0.345$. The correlation is not strong
but the signal is clearly informative and directly usable: weight correspondences
in RANSAC, drop unreliable ones, or send more queries where the model is unsure.
v2 outputs flow only and has nothing comparable.

\subsection{Interactive tool}
A PyQt application loads a video, steps frame by frame, and computes flow for a
region the user drags out. Two modes: exact decoding inside the box against a
full-frame coarse pass, or a cropped mode running the whole pipeline on the
selection so cost scales with the area requested. Both print their latency
breakdown live.

\section{What v3 is for}
The contribution is an operating point, not a leaderboard position. v3 gives v2's
accuracy from a smaller model and adds three properties the fixed-resolution
design cannot express:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Flow at any continuous coordinate, with sparse output identical to dense.
\item Repeat access at 2.55\ms\ per query batch against a cached frame, versus a
      full 19.6\ms\ recomputation.
\item A calibrated error estimate on every correspondence.
\end{enumerate}

If an application consumes one full dense flow field per frame and nothing else,
v2 is the better choice. It produces that product 12\% faster and with higher
sub-pixel precision. v3 is the right choice when the consumer picks its own query
points, revisits a frame, needs positions between pixels, or wants a confidence
value.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Sub-pixel precision.} v3 trails v2 by 0.8 to 6.3 points of 1-pixel
accuracy while matching or beating it on mean error. The decoder makes fewer large
errors but is less exact on most pixels. I know the cause: the decoder's finest
input is at $1/8$ resolution, so the evidence it sees barely changes inside an
$8\times8$ cell, while v2's upsampler reads the full-resolution frame directly.
Adding a Fourier positional encoding of the sub-cell offset changed nothing, which
rules out missing positional signal and points at missing high-resolution
features.

\textbf{Adapted normalisation statistics.} The frozen stack's learned weights are
unchanged, but its batch-normalisation running statistics took roughly 24{,}800
updates during training instead of holding v2's values. Two consequences. The
comparison in Section~4.1 is v3 with statistics adapted to its own training set
against v2 with statistics from FlyingThings, not a decoder-only comparison, and
for the mixed runs that adaptation points at the evaluation domain. Out-of-domain
robustness also suffers: on $1920\times1080$ Spring imagery, far from the
$320\times496$ training crops, the adapted statistics degrade the coarse flow
badly while v2's untouched statistics do not. The training loop is fixed. Runs
with strictly frozen statistics are needed to separate the decoder's contribution
from this effect.

\textbf{No embedded measurement.} Every latency figure is V100 or RTX 4060. The
edge claim is a design target, not a result.

\textbf{One evaluation domain.} VKITTI2 is synthetic driving footage. It says
little about field or survey imagery.

\textbf{Statistical resolution.} One seed per configuration, with
checkpoint-to-checkpoint variation up to 0.038\px. Differences below about
0.05\px\ are not resolved.

\section{Next}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Full-resolution stem.} Give the decoder a cheap full-resolution
      feature map, estimated 1 to 2\ms. This attacks the precision limitation
      directly and makes sub-pixel querying substantive rather than
      interpolative.
\item \textbf{Re-run with frozen statistics}, removing the confound in
      Section~6 and restoring out-of-domain robustness.
\item \textbf{Decode-path optimisation.} The decode is launch-bound. CUDA graph
      capture, fusing the three samplers that share coordinates, and
      \texttt{torch.compile} (measured at 9\% on the coarse pass) together project
      a first query below v2's latency.
\item \textbf{Spring high-resolution evaluation}, testing output above the input
      sampling rate. Blocked on item 2.
\item \textbf{Jetson measurement}, which converts the edge claim into a result or
      refutes it.
\item \textbf{Registration on field imagery}, testing the intended application.
\end{enumerate}
